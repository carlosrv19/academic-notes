Chapter 2: Using surveys studies to gather data#

In this chapter we deep dive in some types of research study. They can be observational or experimental studies.

  • In observational studies the researcher is limited only to record information from subjects (or units). No intervention or manipulation of the process involved in the data generation.

  • In contrast, in experimental studies the researcher purposely manipulates some variables associated with the study. Often, this manipulation is the result of randomly assigning a predefined treatment to the experimental unit. For example, in a clinical trial, the researcher randomly assigns a patient to one of many treatments. In this case, the variable being manipulated is “treatment” that takes values (levels) treatment_A, treatment_B, etc. Experimental studies are executed with maximum control so that if a difference is found between different groups, that difference is attributed only to the fact that groups have different treatments. For that reason, in experimental studies, one group is very similar in characteristics to other group(s), except in that they have been given a different treatment. One common experimental design is the so called completely randomized experiment, where (ideally) a random sample is taken from a population of interest, then a random assignment to a given treatment is done.

Observational studies#

Key terminology#

  • Comparative versus descriptive studies: In comparatives studies, the aim is to identify if a particular method, encoded as a factor or variable, is more effective than other. For example, when several groups are compared based on a given attribute (the factor or variable). These are still observational studies, as the variable that encodes the method, thus defining the groups, is not manipulated by the researcher. Descriptive studies focus on characterizing a population (from a sample) by estimating population parameters.

Observational studies can be further categorized as”

  • Sample survey (cross sectional) studies, whenever the data is collected at a single point in time.

  • Retrospective studies, when a population is analyzed in the present (partial data collected in present time) but additional records are included and analyzed from the past.

  • Prospective studies, when an initial observation (data collection) is performed in the present time, but subjects are followed up and records collected moving forward in time.

  • Types of variables:

    • Response variable is the variable used by the researcher to formulate the research question. By analyzing this variable it is expected to answer the question of interest. This variable is also called dependent, outcome or target variable.

    • Explanatory variables are variables that serve to explain variations in a response variable. They are also called predictor or independent variables. A fundamental difference between observational and experimental studies is that, when an association is found between explanatory and response variables, this association is interpreted as cause and effect for the case of experimental studies, and only as a relationship for the case of observational studies. In other words, “correlation does not imply causation” specially for observational studies.

  • Confounding versus Lurking variables: In general, variables known to have an effect both in explanatory and response variables, are called confounding. These variables are included in the study to “adjust” such effect in the response. Variables having similar association between explanatory and response variables but that are unknown to the researcher (or impractical to include in the study) are refer to as lurking variables.

Sampling design for surveys#

Key definitions#

  • Target population: The complete set of subjects or objects of interest, from which we want to learn the key aspects of the research question.

  • Sample: A subset of the Target population.

  • Sampled population: Corresponds with the collection of subjects or objects from the Target population that are susceptible to be sampled. Ideally, the sampled population is the same as the target population, however, given the complexity of the population or the mechanism to sample objects, it is sometimes impossible that some objects or subjects can actually be sampled. For instance, in a production chain, we may be targeting a finished good produced by a certain machine. The population, “finished goods produced by X machine” has elements that clearly are not even produced today. Finished goods to be produced in a future production cycle, though part of the population, have not potential to be observed at the moment of the sampling procedure.

  • Observation unit: corresponds with the object or subject from which data is collected or observed.

  • Sampling unit: Can be thought of as the physical “container” or entity which is actually selected, and from which observation units are considered.

  • Simple random sample: Is a sample of a predefined size, say \(n,\) that has been selected from a random process such that any sample of size \(n\) had the same chance of having been selected.
